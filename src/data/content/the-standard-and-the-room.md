*Names and identifying details in this post have been fictionalized.*

*What follows happened a few weeks ago. I have had time to sit with it.*

I recently joined a new company as a senior software engineer. Before that I spent three years as an engineering manager, and before that I built software in Silicon Valley and Seattle alongside people who knew exactly what they were doing and held everyone around them to it. I know what good delivery looks like from both sides of the table. I also know what the absence of it looks like.

I wrote about that absence, more or less, a few weeks before this. Then I lived it.

The project I joined specifically to work on is the most important one at the firm. My manager told me early on that nobody else on the project had worked on something this size at the company. The individual features I was hired to build represent a meaningful portion of the largest contract at the firm. The project had been running for months before I arrived. The requirements, as best I could tell on arrival, amounted to: build a dashboard. No PRD. No user stories. No acceptance criteria. A business requirements document that described a desired outcome in the loosest possible terms and nothing beneath it.

I did what any engineer with a pulse would do. I asked for requirements. I kept it professional. I offered structure, linked to templates, proposed a meeting series with documented outcomes. Standard stuff. The response I got from the senior project manager was a question asking me what the expectation on requirements was.

Her signature contained six project management credentials, including a PMP and a SAFe Program Consultant certification.

I want to recount what happened next precisely because the details matter. When I followed up to reinforce the urgency, the service delivery senior manager responded. Not to address the requirements gap. To tell me that her team supported multiple projects and could not be expected to respond within four hours. That I should not communicate in email or Teams but face to face, cameras on, and start building a relationship. Her signature contained four credentials of her own, including an MBA and a Certified Agile Leadership Coach certification.

A Certified Agile Leadership Coach telling a senior engineer to get off the written record on the most important contract at the firm. A SAFe Program Consultant who did not know what to do with a request for user stories. Combined they held more project management credentials than I have ever seen in a single email thread. Combined they had produced, after months of active development, a document that said build a dashboard.

I have written before about credentials without accountability. About passive management filling the vacuum with observation instead of ownership. I did not expect to walk straight into a live demonstration of it.

What I also did not expect was my own manager.

I showed him the thread, the one he was on and had actively ignored. He read my emails and said "this is all true." He read the responses I received and said "this is also true." Then he read my reply, in which I documented the requirements gap as an active blocker for the entire engineering team, named it as a dependency the group had raised previously, and proposed a collaborative path forward through the BA manager, and said "oh . . . this is going to haunt you. Kate is uh." He did not finish the sentence. He never said I was wrong. He never pushed back on the substance. He agreed with every word I wrote and then warned me about the political consequences of having written it. He did not escalate. He did not say anything to anyone. He impotently went back to his day.

I wrote in my last post about what management is actually for. About the manager who shielded his team from a timeline fight they never knew was happening. About showing up before things go sideways and absorbing pressure instead of redirecting it downward. My own manager read a thread where I had done exactly what a senior engineer should do, agreed with all of it, and then demonstrated, in a single conversation, the precise failure mode I had spent three years trying not to be.

I discounted him after that. Not personally. Professionally. He is a political pawn in his own right and I do not think he chose that, but I stopped expecting anything from him that requires spine.

The harder thing to sit with is what happened to my own experience in all of this. Three years as an engineering manager. Before that, years of building software in environments where the standard was high and the people around you made sure you knew it. The experience I bring to this role does not come from a title. I built it alongside teams that earned it together, in places that understood intrinsically what it meant to ship software and hold a bar.

I named that experience directly. I offered it as collaboration. I framed requirements conversations using the same language and structures that had worked across multiple teams and products. People nodded. Then proceeded as if I had not spoken. Not with hostility. With a kind of blank, structural incomprehension that proves more frustrating than disagreement would have been. You cannot debate someone who does not have the frame of reference to understand what they are nodding at. When someone walks in and demonstrates fluency in the work that credentialed people are supposed to own, the threat is not to the project. The threat lands on the people whose job was to have done that work already. Engaging with the experience means acknowledging the gap it exposed. Routing around it is safer.

This is, I have come to think, a company that believes it is an accounting firm that offers software. The truth sits closer to the opposite. It is a software firm that offers accounting, and the growing pains of that transition are real. What is harder to excuse is that nobody with the authority to name it seems to have the will to reckon with what it means. The legacy hierarchy runs deep. Deference to title, credential signaling, process as cover for the absence of outcomes. These are features in an accounting firm. In a software company they are bugs. The missing requirements, the credentialed passivity, the blank nodding, the manager who agrees and does nothing: they all make more sense inside that frame. This is not a broken project. It is an identity crisis at the organizational level.

I have not left. I also have not stopped naming things. The BA manager on the project, when I finally reached him, responded with more accountability in a single email than the rest of the thread had produced in months. He said the requirements were scattered. He said the process had been reactive. He proposed a path forward. We spoke by phone. We aligned. A foundation exists now that did not exist before.

One person, no posturing, no credentials in the signature, moved more in an afternoon than months of certified process management had delivered.

The project is not fixed. The political situation is not resolved. My manager is still my manager. But I have learned something worth adding to everything I wrote before: the environment will tell you where the bar is. It will not tell you where it has to stay.

You can raise the bar in a room that didn't ask you to. What you cannot do is make the room care that you did.
